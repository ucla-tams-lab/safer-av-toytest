Terminal-State-Aware Long-Horizon Sim-to-Real Evaluation: toy study

From short real–simulation paired transitions, we estimate the real cost of a fixed policy over a long goal sequence. Numbers are for the 100 test sequences of length K=8 (536 test sequences in total), averaged over 3 data seeds × 3 model seeds = 9 runs (mean ± std). All learned methods use the same real data and the same training budget.

Main findings
  • Terminal-state correction is needed. Segment-wise outcome correction (SCAPE-style), which corrects only the per-segment cost, has 10–30× the K=8 error of target-only on Safety and Exposure, and beats target-only in 7 of 225 comparisons (5 costs × 5 values of n × 9 runs).
  • The Bellman decomposition is needed. Learning long-sequence costs directly from the same real rollouts (B1, B2) gives 2.9–16× the K=8 error of the direct estimator. B1 beats target-only in 0 of 225 comparisons, B2 in 1.
  • Calibrated simulation data improve on real data alone. Against target-only (the same backward Bellman learning on real data only), the direct estimator wins 201 of 225 comparisons, residual direct 213, and the simulation-backed estimator 162. With n=200 paired transitions, the direct estimator reduces the target-only error by 55% (Safety), 49% (Exposure), 46% (TTC) and 36% (Path).
  • The best estimator depends on the cost (§5). The direct estimator is the most stable across costs and is the main one.
  • At K=1 no correction is needed. Every test sequence starts at rest at a goal, and a single segment from rest is almost the same in real and simulation. At K=1, raw simulation has the lowest error (Safety, Exposure, Path; §4, per-K tables).

1. Problem and method

A fixed goal-conditioned policy executes a goal sequence of K local goals; we estimate its real performance JR. Simulation can be run freely, but the real system is observed only through short segments. Segment-wise outcome correction adds the per-segment cost mismatch. However, the real execution ends each segment in a different state (position and velocity) than the simulation, and that terminal state changes the cost of all remaining goals.

Backward Bellman calibration learns the real continuation value Wθ(x; gk:K) of the remaining-goal sequence, for remaining-goal lengths 1→8. Paired transitions provide paired Bellman targets that continue from the real terminal state; simulated transitions are converted to real targets by the calibration model hφ(x, gk:K, ZS), which adds training data.

Premise. The simulator is a fixed black box. Its parameters cannot be tuned to imitate the real system, and the real–simulation difference is not assumed to be expressible through simulator parameters. System identification and learned real-dynamics models are therefore out of scope; the only operation is to correct simulation outcomes toward the real system.

paired transition :  Ẑ_R = y_R + W_prev(x_R⁺; g_k+1:K),   Ẑ_S = y_S + W_prev(x_S⁺; g_k+1:K)
calibration model :  h_φ(x, g_k:K, Ẑ_S) → Ẑ_R                     (fit on paired transitions)
sim transition    :  Z̃_R = h_φ(x, g_k:K, y_S + W_prev(x_S⁺; g_k+1:K))
continuation value:  W_θ(x; g_k:K) ← Ẑ_R (paired) and Z̃_R (simulation)   (each length L refits all lengths 1..L)
training rows     :  75% paired targets, 25% corrected simulation targets

Proposed estimators ours

Direct (direct amortized estimator): ĴRdirect = Ŵ1(x1; g1:K). No simulation per query.
Sim-backed (simulation-backed estimator): target-sequence simulation rollouts plus the calibrated correction hφ − ẐS at every segment (finite-horizon sim-to-real decomposition).
Residual direct: WR = WS + D, with WS computed by simulation Monte Carlo and only the residual D learned backward. Estimate ĴS + D̂(x1; g1:K).

Baselines baselines

Raw sim: mean simulated cost ĴS.
Seg. outcome (segment-wise outcome correction, Jseg): a learned correction hout(x, g, yS) → yR summed along simulation rollouts. SCAPE-style; ignores the terminal-state mismatch.
Target-only: the same backward Bellman learning of Wθ on the real side of the paired data only, without simulation data. The strongest baseline.
B1 MC-real: direct regression of the observed real return over every window (start, length) of the same real rollouts. No Bellman decomposition and no goal-sequence reuse.
B2 route-level SCAPE: h(x, g, JS) → observed real return on the same windows, with JS from simulating the window's goals. SCAPE applied to whole sequences.

All learned methods use the same n paired transitions plus 50 separate real validation transitions, and the same budget (MLP 3×256; stop after 3,000 gradient steps without validation improvement, at most 30,000; best validation checkpoint).

2. Environment

Maze and policy

A 9×7-cell maze on PointMaze (MuJoCo ball) with 9 local goals A–I fixed at the junctions. A valid goal sequence moves only between adjacent goals and never returns to the previous goal. The policy is a PD controller (kp 2.0, kd 0.4) aiming at the end of the current straight corridor of the shortest path, and sees only the current goal. A segment ends when the ball enters the radius-0.2 ball around the goal; the velocity carries over.

Real vs. simulation

The real system transmits less force (β = 0.85) and brakes much more weakly (brake = 0.4: only 40% of commands opposing the velocity). It therefore reaches junctions at a speed of about 3.9 (simulation about 2.4) and swings wide toward the outer wall in the next turn. The policy is the same in both. This is a Sim2Sim study: the "real" system is the simulator with changed dynamics.

3. Data

Paired transitions from real rollouts

The real system executes random goal sequences of length 2–8 without reset (real chains). At every segment, the simulator is set to the current real state (position and velocity) and executes the same goal once. Half of the chains start at rest at a goal, half at a moving state near a goal. A chain that starts at rest may not share its first 4 goals with a K≥4 test sequence (no leakage). The n paired transitions are the first n of the collection; 50 validation transitions are collected the same way.

Simulation data and test sequences

sim_train (20,000): random position and velocity (0–5) near a goal 60%, simulated arrival states 30%, at rest at a goal 10%; one segment each.
seedR (4,000): one simulated segment from near the real states of the n paired transitions (small noise), where Ŵ is evaluated most.
Test sequences: 100 random sequences each for K = 1, 2, 4, 8 plus 136 K=4 sequences grouped by first edge, all starting at rest. JR is the mean over 100 real rollouts.

Comparison by sequence length K

B1 and B2 can only learn a length-L sequence from a real window of length L under the same real budget. Windows of length 8 number 2–5 per data seed at n=200 and only 16–26 at n=1000. Because of the leakage rule, real windows that start at rest have length at most 3, while every test sequence starts at rest. K=1 is a single straight segment from rest to an adjacent goal; simulation and real are almost identical there, and uncorrected raw simulation is often the most accurate.

The simulation-backed estimator corrects along simulation rollouts with a calibration model that learns the map from simulation values to real values. When the gap is large but structured (Safety, Exposure: ranking preserved), this map is easy to learn. When the gap is small, the situation differs: the real–simulation path-length difference of one segment is 0.09 on average, while the real path length of a K=8 sequence is 20.5 on average. Handling two large quantities (Ŵ and the simulation cost) separately makes small relative errors large compared with the difference. The residual variant computes the large common part WS by simulation and learns only the small difference D, which avoids this. (This is an interpretation; no error-decomposition experiment is included in this study.)

6. Limitations

  • The study is Sim2Sim (real = simulation with changed dynamics). The black-box simulator premise has not been tested on a physical robot or a high-fidelity simulator.
  • The 75% share of paired targets was chosen by looking at test results (a small sweep over 9%, 25%, 50%, 75%). It should be selected on validation data.
  • The residual variant computes WS by multi-segment simulation rollouts from arbitrary states during training, which departs from the short-simulation-data setting.
  • Training and test use the same maze and the same 9 goals; generalization to new mazes or goal locations has not been tested.
  • No single estimator is best on every cost (§5).
  • Paired transitions are collected along real rollouts of several consecutive segments (real chains); this collection assumption should be stated.
  • The dynamics are a PointMaze ball; vehicle dynamics (heading, turning radius) have not been studied.
